# DARKHORSE — Moriah Wilson Episode
## Consolidated format and editorial recommendations

**Working format:** 20–40 minutes, with a preferred target of 25–30 minutes.  
**Positioning:** An evidence-driven investigative thriller assembled from primary sources, supported by clearly labeled cinematic recreations and investigative graphics.  
**Editorial priority:** Make the investigation immediately compelling while keeping Moriah “Mo” Wilson present as a person.

### Basis and scope

This document consolidates the recommendations in the *Review YouTube Videos* conversation and grounds them in the uploaded **DARKHORSE · What the Audience Sees**, dated October 13, 2026, by @AC. Beat numbers below refer to that 34-beat outline.

The outline was read directly for this consolidation. This is an editorial development document, not an independently verified case chronology. Case details, quotations, timestamps, and descriptions of available footage must be checked against the production’s original records before scripting or publication. Illustrative timings are creative targets, not measured source-clip durations.

## 1. Format and source constraints

Let the strength of the material determine the runtime. Use **20–40 minutes as the flexible series range** and **25–30 minutes as the normal target**. The earlier suggestion of approximately 24–28 minutes fits inside this approach; it should not become a hard limit.

Build the episode from the material the production has or can obtain:

- Police body-camera footage.
- Police interviews and interrogations.
- Court documents, exhibits, and discovery.
- 911 calls and other recorded statements.
- Other relevant records obtained through FOIA or applicable public-records processes.

**Do not use personal or family photographs, news clips, or outside archival imagery.** Surveillance, courtroom recordings, booking images, and evidence photographs are candidates only when available within the permitted source collection and approved for production use. Track acquisition and use status separately.

The uploaded outline includes trial photographs of Mo in beats **3, 7, and 34**, despite its opening restriction on personal photographs. Remove those photo treatments under the user’s stated constraint. A photograph’s appearance in a court exhibit does not override that production rule.

Family words already documented in court records can still support text cards or authentic courtroom audio, where available. They do not require family photographs or new interviews.

## 2. Four visual and audio categories

| Category | Material | Editorial purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **Authentic footage** | Bodycam, police interviews, permitted surveillance and courtroom recordings | Let real discoveries, exchanges, and decisions carry credibility and tension. |
| **Authentic audio** | 911, recorded interviews, witness statements, permitted courtroom audio | Convey testimony and emotion; carry scenes when the corresponding picture is unavailable. |
| **Cinematic recreations** | Clearly labeled environments, first-person perspectives, and documented spatial reconstructions | Explain geography, movement, or otherwise unseen context. |
| **Investigative graphics** | Maps, timelines, relationship charts, document excerpts, evidence comparisons, communications | Make the evidence and its connections understandable. |

Authentic material can retain its raw appearance. Recreations can be composed and cinematic. Graphics connect the two. Viewers must always be able to distinguish a recording from a reconstruction.

## 3. Narrative engine: the audience knows the suspect

For this episode, establish Kaitlin Armstrong early. Build suspense around **how investigators connected her to the evidence, why she was released, how she disappeared, and how the pursuit reached her**.

This approach avoids spending a large part of the episode concealing a suspect whose interview and flight provide the strongest immediate hook. It also gives the relationships, vehicle evidence, and later forensic findings a clear purpose when they appear.

Introduce her as the suspect at the relevant stage of the investigation; distinguish contemporaneous suspicion from the later verdict. Her silence or request to leave should not be presented as proof of guilt.

Preserve the outline’s **“what police know / what we know”** device, introduced in beat 6 and updated throughout. Make the second column explicitly mean **what the audience has been shown**. Date the investigation’s knowledge where possible, and use later records to support retrospective reveals. Do not imply investigators knew something earlier than the records establish.

The panel should appear when it clarifies a meaningful difference, rather than becoming a permanent screen element. Its convergence at capture in beat 30 remains a useful structural payoff, provided it does not suggest every evidentiary question was resolved at that moment.

## 4. Rebuild the opening as a 90-second cold open

The current opening progresses through the 911 call, police arrival, Mo’s photograph, the hidden bicycle, Colin’s name, and finally Kaitlin’s interview. The main conflict arrives at beat 6.

Move that conflict forward. Deliver three major developments—**the vehicle connection, release, and disappearance**—then leave the audience with an investigative question.

| Time | Picture and sound | Audience learns |
|---|---|---|
| **0:00–0:15** | A concise, accurately contextualized excerpt from Kaitlin’s police interview concerning the vehicle and her departure. | Police are questioning a woman about a vehicle linked to the scene. |
| **0:15–0:35** | Actual surveillance and a minimal timeline using the lock record and camera evidence from beats 13–16. | Mo’s return and the vehicle’s recorded appearance are close in time. |
| **0:35–0:55** | The interview exchange and a concise record-supported explanation of the warrant issue. | Kaitlin is released despite the investigators’ questions. |
| **0:55–1:15** | The documented vehicle sale and a restrained route graphic, drawn from beats 23–25. | She sells the vehicle and leaves the area. |
| **1:15–1:30** | Hold on the central evidence connection; title, then move directly into the investigation. | The episode will explain how that connection became a case and an international pursuit. |

**Suggested closing question:** How did investigators connect the woman they released to Mo’s killing—and find her after she disappeared?

Use only the strongest excerpts needed to make those developments understandable. Do not splice separate interview moments into an apparently continuous exchange. If the authentic exchange needs more room, adjust the surrounding narration rather than distorting it.

**Chronology correction for development:** The earlier conversation’s sample narration said “the night before” and “the following day.” The outline identifies a May 11 crime, a May 12 phone-data event, and a May 13 sale; it does not independently settle every interview and travel date. Use verified dates in the final script. Likewise, treat the outline’s 8:36 and 8:37 timestamps as source-check items, including any camera-clock offsets. Do not equate the time of discovery with the time of death.

## 5. Revised act structure

The outline currently moves through **The Night → Mo → The Last Evening → The Gun → The Sale → The Ad → Close**. Its main pacing risk is the early pause for biography and relationship history after the opening.

Use this episode-specific structure as a starting cut:

| Section | Target | Story function and outline material |
|---|---:|---|
| **Cold open** | 1:30 | Vehicle connection, interview, release, disappearance. Select from beats 6, 13–16, 23–25. |
| **1. The Last 60 Minutes** | 4:00 | Establish the crime, Mo’s reason for being in Austin, and the critical evening timeline. Draw from beats 1–5, 9, 13–17. |
| **2. The Other Woman** | 4:00 | Explain the relationships, communications, and attributed statements needed to understand the investigation. Beats 10–12, 19, 24. |
| **3. The Evidence** | 5:00 | Connect the vehicle, weapon evidence, phone records, and interview. Beats 18, 20–22, 26. |
| **4. The Disappearance** | 4:00 | Develop the sale, travel, and identity evidence beyond the cold-open tease. Beats 23, 25, 27 and relevant portions of 28. |
| **5. The Hunt** | 4:00 | Explain the Costa Rica search, recruitment-ad operation, and capture. Beats 28–30. |
| **6. The Reckoning** | 3:00 | Briefly cover the later escape attempt if it adds value, then the verdict, sentence, and Mo’s life and family’s words. Beats 31–34, with material from 7–8. |
| **Total** | **25:30** | A starting allocation within the 25–30-minute target. |

“The Last 60 Minutes” is a working title: use **“The Final Evening”** if the verified interval or selected material does not fit that claim.

For the repeatable series template, these functions can be grouped into **cold open → incident → investigation → breakthrough → pursuit → resolution**. They are functions, not mandatory chapter counts. The Moriah Wilson episode benefits from separating the disappearance and hunt.

Distribute biography where it gives a discovery human meaning. Establish Mo’s name, age, cycling career, and reason for visiting Austin early; add the fuller portrait in short, relevant passages and the ending. Avoid reducing her to either a victim introduction or a final memorial.

## 6. Pacing and narrative density

**The footage serves the story.** Possessing a long interrogation does not require using it at length. An eight-minute exchange may contain 45 essential seconds; a 45-minute recording may contribute only three minutes to the finished film.

Evaluate **meaningful story development per minute**, not cuts per minute:

1. **Aim for a change in understanding every 30–60 seconds.** A discovery, contradiction, consequence, or newly sharpened question counts. Treat this as a diagnostic, not a mechanical quota.
2. **Use each source clip for a clear purpose.** Identify the fact, emotion, or decision it uniquely delivers.
3. **Avoid saying the same thing three times.** Narration, a recording, and a graphic should each add something rather than repeat one point.
4. **Preserve exceptional real moments.** Let a compelling exchange breathe when the tension is in the recording itself.
5. **Use graphics only when they help.** A document highlight or vehicle comparison should make a connection easier to see.
6. **Make recreations do work.** They should explain documented movement, space, or context, rather than pad transitions.
7. **Keep emotional pauses.** The silence in beat 17 and the closing return to Mo can be effective without a new revelation every few seconds.

For example, compress a three-minute relationship explanation into a purposeful **60–90-second progression**: Colin’s relevant statement → relationship timeline → an accurately attributed witness account → Kaitlin’s vehicle interview. Retain qualifications and context; do not turn a witness’s allegation into an established fact.

A useful recurring sequence is **setup → primary-source moment → necessary interpretation → next question**. Omit interpretation when the recording already makes the point clearly.

## 7. AI recreations: uses and ethical boundaries

Treat AI recreation as **cinematic visualization of documented information**.

| Application | Treatment | Boundary |
|---|---|---|
| **The final evening** | Pool, streets, bicycle, apartment geography, and map-based movement. | Establish routes and times from records; do not invent conversations or exact gestures. |
| **The surveillance context** | A labeled spatial reconstruction leading into authentic camera footage. | Keep the transition explicit; reconstructed movement must not appear to fill an evidentiary gap with proof. |
| **The disappearance** | Airport environments, route maps, restrained first-person details. | Do not fabricate a readable passport, boarding pass, or other document. |
| **The capture** | Documented geography and approach to the location, followed by records or authentic footage. | Verify the ad, operation, and location before producing a literal reconstruction. |
| **Mo’s cycling and the ending** | Labeled, evocative first-person riding imagery. | Present it as an illustrative tribute when no record establishes that exact ride or viewpoint. |

Label reconstructions visibly and use a distinct visual treatment. Avoid synthetic dialogue, cloned case-participant voices, invented behavior, or imagery that appears to document the killing. Do not imply access to a person’s private thoughts through a first-person shot.

Keep recreated screens and advertisements labeled as graphic recreations. Use verbatim wording only where the source supports it. Atmosphere and sound design must not be mistaken for authentic recorded evidence.

Preserve the outline’s restraint around the crime scene: **do not show Mo’s body**. Preserve the intended final movement from verdict to family testimony to a labeled Vermont riding sequence, replacing the final personal photograph with the riding image, her name, or a restrained text card.

## 8. Research database and evidence links

Organize the existing drive into five connected datasets. Give records stable IDs so every narrative claim and production asset can be traced back to its source.

| Dataset | Core fields |
|---|---|
| **Events** | Event ID; date/time and precision; time zone; location; participants; description; supporting evidence IDs; unresolved conflicts. |
| **Evidence** | Evidence ID; original filename/path; source agency or proceeding; document page or recording timecode; transcript; claim supported; verification status; acquisition/use status. |
| **People** | Person ID; name; role; relationships; attributed statements; linked events and evidence. |
| **Narrative beats** | Beat ID; story question; audience learns; new development; supporting evidence IDs; estimated duration; act; outstanding checks. |
| **Visual assets** | Asset ID; category; source clip in/out points; linked beat; graphic or recreation brief; labeling requirement; use status; production status. |

Keep **event time**, **time investigators learned something**, and **time the audience learns it** separate. This supports the two-column graphic and prevents an edited chronology from becoming misleading.

Example beat: **Police release Kaitlin Armstrong.** Link the interview recording, timestamped transcript, relevant warrant documentation, police report, and subsequent timeline. Those links should support an event summary, selected sound bite, narration draft, visual plan, and fact-check citation. If the reason for release is not established by the clip alone, supply the appropriate record rather than infer it from the edit.

## 10. Production next steps

1. **Audit the outline and source inventory.** Remove the prohibited photo treatments; confirm which recordings, exhibits, and transcripts actually exist in the drive.
2. **Build the verified chronology.** Reconcile interview, sale, flight, camera, phone, and lock-record times. Resolve the cold-open date language before recording narration.
3. **Select authentic moments.** Log precise in/out points for the interview, 911, bodycam discoveries, vehicle footage, and sale. Record why each selection earns its runtime.
4. **Create the linked beat sheet.** Give every beat a new development, supporting source, visual treatment, and duration estimate.
5. **Assemble the opening five minutes first.** Test whether the 90-second hook creates a clear question and the return to the crime scene sustains momentum.
6. **Set the narration balance using that assembly.** The conversation left continuous narration versus sparse narration unresolved. Start with real recordings carrying decisive moments and narration supplying necessary context; assess the result in the pilot sequence.
7. **Prototype the visual system.** Test the knowledge panel, timeline, vehicle comparison, source labels, and recreation labels before building the full graphics package.
8. **Produce only the recreations the cut needs.** Attach a source-backed brief and clear labeling to each one.
9. **Cut toward 25–30 minutes, then review density and clarity.** Remove repetition before removing substantive evidence. Extend toward 40 minutes only where material earns the time.
10. **Complete factual and editorial review.** Check every claim, quote, timestamp, evidence comparison, and reconstruction against its source; confirm the ending returns attention to Mo without relying on prohibited imagery.

### Working editorial brief

**DARKHORSE lets viewers follow an investigation through real evidence. Open with the vehicle connection, release, and disappearance; reconstruct how the case developed; let authentic moments carry tension; use graphics and labeled recreations to make the evidence understandable; end with the person whose life was lost.**
